THE BISONS BEAT | DANIEL MERCER | 2027 SEASON

A Seat for Patricia, a Victory for Oklahoma City: The Bison's First Sunday Was About More Than Football

The Bison defeated Tennessee 21–14 in their inaugural regular-season game. But before the first snap, Oklahoma City witnessed a moment that may outlast anything that happens on the field this season.

OKC 1–0

Week 1 • Oklahoma City, Oklahoma

There are 60 minutes in an NFL football game.

On Sunday afternoon in Oklahoma City, the most important moment happened before the clock ever started.

Before Will Howard took his first regular-season snap as the quarterback of the Oklahoma City Bison. Before the defense intercepted Tennessee three times. Before the franchise recorded its first victory under a new name, in a new city, before a fanbase that had spent months imagining what this day might feel like.

There was a chair.

One extraordinary chair.

And a son who wanted his mother to have the best seat in the house.

Patricia's Seat

High above the field, on the stadium's 50-yard line, in the very front row of the premier midfield seating section, sits Section 200, Row A, Seat 1.

Not a luxury suite. Not a private owner's box tucked behind tinted glass.

A seat with an unobstructed view of the entire playing surface, close enough to hear the crowd rise and positioned perfectly to see every formation, every touchdown, every moment.

It is the best seat in the stadium.

And it will forever belong to Patricia Luong.

The chair itself is unlike any other in the building. Its frame is finished in polished bronze, with dark walnut armrests and intricate engravings worked into the metal. The Bison emblem appears beneath the headrest, surrounded by delicate floral detailing.

A small inscription beneath the seat number reads:

PATRICIA LUONG

Forever Loved. Forever Remembered.

The Best Seat in the House.

Below it, another engraving:

Every dream begins with someone who believed in you.

It is an unusual monument in professional sports. Stadiums generally memorialize championship coaches, franchise quarterbacks and wealthy benefactors.

This one honors a mother.

And that distinction matters.

Bryan Luong, the Bison's controlling owner, grew up in Renton, Washington. His relationship with Seattle football helped shape the philosophy that eventually became Oklahoma City's organizational identity: Always Compete.

But behind the football ambitions, the trades, the relocation and the relentless pursuit of a championship was a much more personal story.

His mother would not get to see the franchise he built.

So he made certain that, in a way, she always would.

The unveiling

The stadium was already filling when the pregame ceremony began.

The giant video boards turned away from the usual player introductions and advertisements. Instead, they displayed a simple message.

FOR PATRICIA.

The noise inside the stadium gradually faded.

On the video boards, the camera settled on the covered seat. A ceremonial navy-and-orange cloth was carefully lifted away, revealing the bronze finish beneath the afternoon light.

For several seconds, there was no music.

No fireworks.

No attempt to turn the moment into a spectacle.

Just the chair, its engravings, and a stadium beginning to understand what it represented.

Then the applause began.

It grew slowly at first, spreading from the surrounding sections until thousands of fans were standing.

Some were wiping their eyes.

Others held their children a little closer.

On the Bison sideline, players watched the video boards in silence.

Bobby Wagner, who has become both a trusted executive and a close friend to Luong, stood with his hands clasped in front of him.

Pete Carroll, normally incapable of remaining quiet for more than a few minutes, said nothing.

Even Scot McCloughan, whose skepticism has become a fixture of the organization's internal culture, watched without his customary commentary.

The applause continued long after the covering had been removed.

It was not the loudest moment of the afternoon.

It may have been the most meaningful.

A different kind of franchise identity

The NFL is a business that measures nearly everything.

Salary-cap efficiency. Draft capital. Player production. Television ratings. Revenue per available seat.

Bryan Luong has demonstrated that he understands the competitive side of that business, sometimes to a degree that makes even his own football executives uncomfortable.

He traded away established stars. He accumulated draft picks and then spent them aggressively. He committed substantial money to free agents. He assembled a roster that entered its first Oklahoma City season among the league's most highly regarded.

Yet Patricia's Seat reveals something that spreadsheets cannot adequately measure.

For all the ambition surrounding this franchise, its owner has also made room for remembrance.

That does not erase the pain Indianapolis experienced when the Colts departed. It does not absolve every difficult decision made during the relocation. Those stories remain part of this organization's history.

But it does complicate the portrait of an owner who has sometimes been characterized as impatient, impulsive or excessively ambitious.

There is a difference between wanting to build something impressive and wanting to build something that lasts.

On Sunday, Luong offered a glimpse of the latter.

And then his football team took the field.

The Bison win. Their quarterback survives. Their defense delivers.

The final score suggests a relatively comfortable victory.

The numbers tell a more complicated story.

Will Howard, the quarterback whose extraordinary 2026 turnaround helped transform this franchise, completed 20 of 35 passes for 168 yards.

No touchdowns. No interceptions. Three sacks. A passer rating of 69.7.

For a team projected to win the Super Bowl, that is hardly the offensive explosion its supporters envisioned.

But Howard did something that matters enormously for a young quarterback: he avoided the catastrophic mistake.

He also contributed 15 rushing yards and a rushing touchdown.

Oklahoma City's offense found its identity on the ground.

Jadarian Price carried 19 times for 81 yards and a touchdown. Trey Benson added 58 yards on just five carries, including a 37-yard run, and scored once himself.

Together with Howard, the Bison rushed for 153 yards and three touchdowns.

Yet Price's two recorded fumbles deserve attention, even though the team finished with zero turnovers.

Last season, he fumbled seven times.

One game into 2027, the concern has not disappeared.

Shane Steichen will have to decide how long he can tolerate that risk with Benson averaging 11.6 yards per carry in the opener.

There were encouraging signs from the passing game.

Rookie Jeremiah Smith led Oklahoma City with four receptions for 62 yards. Tetairoa McMillan added 44 yards, Tyler Warren contributed 36, and Parker Washington caught four passes for 31.

The talent is evident.

The explosive passing attack, at least for one Sunday, remains a work in progress.

The defense refuses to lose

If Howard managed the game, Oklahoma City's defense won it.

The Bison intercepted Tennessee three times.

DeMarvion Overshown recorded an interception with a 26-yard return. Rookie Ashton Hampton added another, returning it 10 yards. Cam Taylor-Britt also registered an interception.

For Hampton, the No. 19 overall selection, it was an immediate return on an expensive draft-day investment.

For the organization, it was validation of its decision to build a secondary deep enough that even established veterans would have to compete for playing time.

The pass rush contributed three sacks.

Caden Curry led the team with 1.5. JT Tuimoloau, Billy Bowman Jr. and Yaya Diaby each contributed half a sack.

And then there was Danny Stutsman.

Nine tackles. One pass defended.

The former Oklahoma linebacker, playing in front of the same state that watched him develop in Norman, led the Bison in tackles during their first regular-season victory.

It was a fitting performance on an afternoon already filled with local significance.

The Titans managed 298 offensive yards. They were not completely shut down, and Oklahoma City allowed 14 points in the third quarter.

That matters. Championship defenses must eventually demonstrate that they can sustain their performance across four quarters.

But the Bison forced the mistakes that mattered most.

They won the turnover battle 3–0.

They won the game 21–14.

And they gave Oklahoma City its first regular-season NFL victory.

What happened afterward

As the final seconds expired, the stadium erupted.

Players embraced near midfield. The Bison sideline spilled toward the field. Fans remained standing, soaking in the moment.

But the most compelling image of the afternoon was not a player celebrating.

It was Patricia's Seat.

Still there.

The bronze catching the stadium lights.

The field stretching out below it.

For the first time, the seat had witnessed a Bison victory.

One could imagine that it would become a tradition for fans to glance toward Section 200 after important moments. A reminder that this franchise's story is about more than its record.

In the locker room, the football questions would soon return.

Howard must become more efficient. Price must protect the ball. The offense must find ways to turn its remarkable collection of receiving talent into consistent production.

The defense cannot reasonably expect three interceptions every Sunday.

And one victory does not validate a Super Bowl prediction.

But those are tomorrow's concerns.

For one afternoon, Oklahoma City had its team, its first victory, and a moment of remembrance that gave the occasion a depth no scoreboard could capture.

The view from Section 200

I've covered professional football long enough to know that opening-day optimism can be misleading.

A talented roster can disappoint. A promising quarterback can regress. A franchise that appears destined for greatness can discover how unforgiving the NFL becomes once expectations arrive.

I still have questions about Bryan Luong's aggressive approach to team-building. I still believe the price paid for some of Oklahoma City's draft acquisitions deserves scrutiny.

And after watching Sunday's offense, I am not prepared to declare the Bison the best team in football.

But I also know that not every important judgment about an organization can be made by examining its transactions.

On Sunday, the Bison gave their new city a victory.

Their defense gave their quarterback room to grow.

And their owner gave his mother a place in the stadium that no future roster move, coaching change or disappointing season can take away.

Long after the details of this 21–14 victory have faded, people may still remember the moment that chair was unveiled.

Perhaps that is the most important thing Oklahoma City built this offseason.

Not the receiving corps.

Not the defense.

Not even the stadium itself.

But a place where the people who helped make the dream possible can remain part of it.

Patricia Luong never got to watch her son's NFL team play.

On Sunday, her seat was waiting anyway.

— Daniel Mercer, The Bison Beat
