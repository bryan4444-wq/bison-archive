Cold open — Two cities, one franchise

The screen opens on two very different landscapes: the familiar football geography of Indianapolis and the wide-open horizon of Oklahoma City. The Colts name carries decades of memory, but the people in the building are preparing for a different future. This retrospective is about the season that connected those two places—and about the people asked to live through it.

Archival narration traces a club that finished 8–9 after a start that made even eight wins seem impossible. The final record is not a neat ending. It is a reminder that a team can improve dramatically while the city that supported it still loses something irreplaceable.

Chapter One — Renton, Patricia, and the roots of a football life

The story begins far from Indiana. Bryan Luong's connection to football runs through the Pacific Northwest and through memories of his mother, Patricia. Seattle is not merely a former hometown in this story; it is part of the reason the game means something personal to the owner who will eventually relocate an NFL franchise.

The film resists the easy mythology of a billionaire origin story. Financial success made the purchase possible, but the personal history explains why football was never just another asset. Patricia's memory later becomes part of the architecture of the new franchise through Patricia's Seat at Love's Field.

Rather than invent a private family conversation, the camera holds on the public consequence: a memorial place that will outlast any single win or loss.

Chapter Two — Buying the Colts, choosing Oklahoma City

The acquisition of the Indianapolis Colts begins as a business event and becomes a civic rupture. Oklahoma City sees the possibility of a first NFL franchise. Indianapolis sees the departure of an institution that has been woven into its Sundays since 1984.

The documentary examines the uncomfortable duality without asking viewers to choose a single emotion. Excitement in one city does not cancel grief in the other. A new ownership group, including Oklahoma-connected investors and a small stake for Shai Gilgeous-Alexander, begins shaping the Bison identity.

Love's Field is still a promise rather than an opening-day backdrop. The inaugural 2027 season will start with preseason home dates at Oklahoma's stadium before the new home is ready for its Week 2 debut.

Chapter Three — The teardown that shocked the league

The most visible statement of the new regime comes through a trade with Seattle. Jonathan Taylor and Quenton Nelson—the kind of players supporters expect to define a team—head west. Arden Key and Riley Leonard are part of the outgoing package as well.

Coming back are Jadarian Price, Uchenna Nwosu, Christian Haynes, Drew Lock, and future draft capital: a 2027 first, a 2027 third and a 2028 second. It is not a simple exchange of talent. It is an exchange of timelines. Seattle is buying a present; the future Bison are buying choices.

More transactions follow. Daniel Jones goes to Pittsburgh in a package that brings Will Howard to Indianapolis. DeForest Buckner and Charvarius Ward move to Atlanta. The roster changes faster than a traditional coaching staff would prefer, and the decisions become a recurring point of tension between ownership, football operations and the coaches.

The film's editorial question is not whether every deal won an imaginary trade calculator. It is whether the organization can make a team out of players acquired from so many different directions.

Chapter Four — Seven losses, then a quarterback

The season opens 0–7 with Will Levis leading the offense. At that point, the relocation story threatens to swallow the football story altogether. Every press conference can be interpreted as a farewell, and every loss as evidence that the rebuild has come at too high a cost.

Then Will Howard takes over. Ten starts later, he is 8–2. His line—210 completions on 325 attempts, 2,403 yards, 14 touchdowns, five interceptions and a 94.6 passer rating—does not require documentary exaggeration. The turnaround is startling on its own.

Tetairoa McMillan emerges as a central target. Tory Horton and Andrei Iosivas become part of the chemistry that Howard will later worry about losing. Jadarian Price approaches a thousand rushing yards, though his seven fumbles complicate the evaluation. The offensive line has its own uneven story: Matt Goncalves allows two sacks, while Robert Jones is charged with thirteen.

The team finishes 8–9. There is no playoff appearance, no triumphant final championship parade. Yet a quarterback has arrived, and the franchise that looked directionless in October suddenly has a football identity to carry west.

Chapter Five — The last Indianapolis Sunday

The documentary slows down for the farewell. Stadium workers, ticket holders and people whose jobs are tied to the team face the consequences of a move that cannot be undone by a better draft class. The organization provides six months of severance to affected employees and prepares farewell gifts and packages for supporters.

Those gestures matter, but the film does not treat them as payment in full for civic loss. A jersey can be kept. A stadium ritual cannot simply be mailed to another address. The final Sunday belongs as much to the people in the stands as to anyone on the field.

The Colts' history does not disappear when the Bison logo is unveiled. The archive preserves both the old record and the uncomfortable fact that the franchise's new chapter was made possible by closing another.

Chapter Six — What comes next

Mina's retrospective closes on a contrast: the finality of Indianapolis and the uncertainty of Oklahoma City. Bobby Wagner, Scot McCloughan, Pete Carroll and Shane Steichen inherit a football operation with a young quarterback, a stockpile of aggressive decisions and a standard that has yet to be tested in its new home.

The last images do not promise a championship. They show the work ahead: a roster to build, a stadium to open and a community to earn. The series' established closing line appears on screen: “The Indianapolis Colts are gone. The Oklahoma City Bison are coming.”
