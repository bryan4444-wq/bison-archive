THE BISON TRIBUNE / PRESEASON REACTION

WHAT THE HELL DID I JUST WATCH?

Three games. Three demolitions. And somehow the Oklahoma City Bison are now Super Bowl favorites.

I have checked the scoreboard three times

I have spent most of the last year arguing that the Oklahoma City Bison deserved to be taken seriously. I gave their offseason an A-minus. I projected 10 or 11 wins. I wrote that Will Howard had earned the opportunity to lead this franchise, and that Bryan Luong's aggressive personnel department had constructed a team with a legitimate ceiling.

I would like the record to show that I did not predict whatever the hell this was.

Thirty-one to ten against Seattle. Forty-two to seven against San Francisco. Twenty-one to nothing against the Rams. Three games, three wins, 94 points scored, 17 surrendered. A plus-77 point differential. Against three NFC West teams. That is not a preseason résumé. That is an extremely impolite introduction.

And now Madden's season preview has the Bison winning Super Bowl LXII over Detroit. I am looking at a prediction graphic that places the Oklahoma City Bison next to the Lombardi Trophy, and I am still trying to reconcile it with the team that began last season 0–7.

Seattle: the first warning

The opener was already going to be emotionally loaded. Seattle is the city of Luong's football upbringing and the organization that acquired Jonathan Taylor and Quenton Nelson during the Colts' teardown. It is also the team that went on to win Super Bowl LXI.

Oklahoma City beat the Seahawks 31–10.

Preseason football requires disclaimers about personnel packages, snap counts and who is even trying to win the fourth quarter. Those disclaimers are valid. They do not change the fact that the first public performance of the relocated franchise was a 21-point win over the defending champions. If the Bison wanted attention, they received it.

San Francisco: this stopped looking accidental

One impressive preseason result can be waved away. A second can still be rationalized. Forty-two to seven against San Francisco is the point at which I stopped searching for a clever caveat and started wondering whether the rest of the league should be genuinely concerned.

Thirty-five points of separation is absurd in any context. The Bison did not merely survive their second exhibition. They overwhelmed their opponent. We do not have the play-by-play or individual box scores in this archive, so I am not going to manufacture a quarterback stat line or pretend I know which defensive player caused each collapse.

But the team-level conclusion is unavoidable: through two games, Oklahoma City had scored 73 points and allowed 17. That is the kind of margin that turns an intriguing new franchise into a national talking point.

The Rams: the defense makes its own argument

The third game was a 21–0 shutout of Los Angeles. Zero. The Rams did not put a point on the board.

Offense gets the headlines when a team scores 42. Defense gets the last word when a team allows none. Yaya Diaby, Leonard Williams, Sauce Gardner, Ashton Hampton and the rest of this defensive group entered the preseason with a significant reputation on paper. A shutout does not prove they will dominate regular-season starters, but it gives that reputation an on-field foundation.

Consider the progression: ten points allowed, then seven, then zero. The offense did not need to score forty to finish the job. That matters.

The Super Bowl prediction is the part I cannot get past

Let me be precise. Oklahoma City has not won a regular-season game as the Bison. It has not played a playoff game. The season-preview simulation is a forecast, not a result. Its bracket projects the Bison as AFC South champions, the AFC's top seed and ultimately Super Bowl LXII champions over the Detroit Lions.

That is an astonishing amount of confidence to place in a relocated franchise whose quarterback has ten career starts in this story and whose roster has been rebuilt at a speed that would make most front offices dizzy.

More baffling still, the preview's Coach of the Year tile features Bryan Luong. Luong is the controlling owner, not the head coach; Shane Steichen holds that job. In the Bison universe, the award is best understood as a projected GM or executive honor, because the game offers no dedicated general-manager award in this presentation. It is a prediction of front-office recognition, not a claim that Luong is coaching the team.

Three preseason games should not make anyone order championship rings. But if I were forced to explain why a simulation might have fallen in love with Oklahoma City, I could point to 94 points scored, 17 allowed and three consecutive convincing wins. The machine has apparently decided it has seen enough. I have not. I am, however, substantially less comfortable dismissing it than I was a month ago.

What I was wrong about—and what I still need to see

My April assessment worried about Will Howard's chemistry with a new receiving group, the cost of the draft trades and whether the defensive talent would function as a coherent unit. Those were fair questions. The preseason did not answer all of them, but it made the team's readiness look considerably less theoretical.

The personnel department also kept moving. Taylor Rapp and Tyson Campbell joined the defensive backfield, while a crowded Week 4 transaction ledger recorded signings, releases and practice-squad assignments. This is not a finished roster in the museum sense. It is a living team, and the front office has shown no hesitation about changing it.

I still want to see Howard against regular-season pressure, the line against a full defensive game plan and the defense against an offense that refuses to show its hand in August. I still believe the cost of acquiring this much talent deserves scrutiny. A preseason sweep cannot retroactively make every trade efficient.

But the old question—can Oklahoma City become respectable?—is dead. Buried under 94 points.

My revised position: I am officially shook

I said 10 or 11 wins. That was an optimistic forecast when I wrote it. After this preseason, it might sound conservative, which is a sentence I never expected to type about this organization.

I am not revising my prediction to 17–0. I am not declaring a championship. And I am absolutely not letting Bryan Luong frame a simulated Super Bowl win as proof of anything that has happened on a football field in January.

Still: the defending champions lost by 21. San Francisco lost by 35. Los Angeles was shut out. The Bison are undefeated in their first preseason and are the game's projected Super Bowl winners before they have played a single regular-season snap in Oklahoma City.

I am a journalist. I am supposed to be skeptical. I am skeptical. I am also, against my better judgment, staring at this team and thinking: what if the prediction is not ridiculous?

Welcome to the Oklahoma City Bison era. Apparently, subtlety was never part of the plan.
