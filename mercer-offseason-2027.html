From Relocation Experiment to Legitimate Contender

The Bison’s First Offseason Makes a Statement

DANIEL MERCER · INDEPENDENT BISON BEAT · APRIL 2027 · RECONSTRUCTED EDITION

There was a time, not particularly long ago, when the biggest question surrounding this franchise was whether Oklahoma City could support an NFL team. Now the question is whether Oklahoma City can win a Super Bowl.

That shift in expectations tells you almost everything about the first offseason under Bryan Luong. A franchise that spent much of 2026 looking toward the exit from Indianapolis now enters its inaugural Oklahoma City campaign looking toward January. The transformation has been rapid, expensive and, at times, startling.

Free agency: an unmistakable declaration

Yaya Diaby gives the Bison a proven edge threat. Parker Washington adds another dangerous option to a receiving room already built around Tetairoa McMillan. Leonard Williams brings veteran credibility to the interior and a familiar face for president of football operations Bobby Wagner. Ikem Ekwonu gives the offensive line another option as the team sorts out its right side.

The signings are evidence of an owner who does not intend to spend the franchise's first years explaining why the team is still building. There is real upside here. There is also a price, and future flexibility always deserves a place in the conversation.

The draft: star power, but not free

Jeremiah Smith at No. 6 is the kind of talent capable of changing a passing attack. Trevor Goosby at No. 14 addresses the trenches. Ashton Hampton at No. 19 brings athleticism and competition to a secondary already featuring Sauce Gardner and Cam Taylor-Britt. The Bison added further upside with Damon Wilson II, Nyck Harbor, Rasheem Biles, Isaiah Sategna and John Mateer.

Those picks did not materialize out of nowhere. Oklahoma City surrendered meaningful assets to move up, including Laiatu Latu in the Arizona deal and multiple selections in the Detroit trade. A strong class can justify that cost, but the price is part of the grade.

The Will Howard question

Howard's 2026 story was remarkable: an 0–7 team, a quarterback change, and an 8–2 run to finish 8–9. His ten starts produced 2,403 passing yards, 14 touchdowns and five interceptions. It was enough to earn the job and the trust of the organization. It was not enough to establish what he can do over a full season with every opponent preparing for him.

With McMillan, Washington, Smith, Harbor and Tyler Warren, he will not lack weapons. But chemistry cannot be bought wholesale. The losses of Tory Horton and Andrei Iosivas, two receivers with whom Howard had developed relationships, underline that point. So do the questions along the offensive line.

Defense and the larger gamble

The secondary has talent and the pass rush has teeth. Diaby and Williams raise the floor of the front. Still, a strong list of names is not the same thing as a fully integrated defense, and depth at linebacker and along the interior could become important over a long season.

The more interesting question may be organizational. Luong has shown he will spend, trade and take risks. Wagner provides credibility and a relationship with the locker room; Scot McCloughan is a counterweight to impulsiveness; Pete Carroll supplies the franchise's competitive mantra; Shane Steichen must turn all of it into weekly execution.

Oklahoma City has reasons to be excited. Indianapolis has reasons to feel conflicted. Neither emotion will block a pass rusher in November.

The numbers that explain the urgency

The Bison do not arrive at this point because the 2026 season went according to plan. They arrive because it went badly enough to force a change, and then unexpectedly well enough to create a temptation. An 0–7 opening gave way to an 8–2 finish under Will Howard. That is not a conventional rebuilding curve. It is an invitation to accelerate.

Acceleration is seductive. It can also confuse the distinction between having a promising quarterback and having a complete team. Oklahoma City has chosen to test that distinction immediately, and there will be no hiding from the results.

What the trade ledger reveals

The most revealing offseason deals were not isolated acts of ambition. The move with Arizona sent Laiatu Latu and picks No. 27 and No. 110 away for No. 6 and Zaven Collins. Detroit received Nos. 32, 52 and 142 in exchange for No. 19. The organization effectively turned quantity into a smaller number of high-profile swings.

That strategy gives Oklahoma City Jeremiah Smith and Ashton Hampton, two players who could become foundational. It also narrows the margin for error. When a team spends several selections to acquire one, that one must eventually be more than a promising name on draft night.

The uncomfortable chemistry calculation

The 2026 offense did not become functional merely because Howard replaced Levis. It became functional through repetition: route timing, protection calls, the recognition of pressure, the small habits that are almost invisible until a team loses them.

Howard's concern about Tory Horton and Andrei Iosivas leaving is therefore more than sentimentality. It is a football observation. He has been given a more talented group on paper, but he must learn how those receivers run, how they adjust and how they respond when the first read disappears. His approved players-only throwing session is a sensible first step, though it remains ahead of us rather than in the books.

The first preseason reality check

The preseason Week 1 roster lists 79 players against a limit of 75. The team is rated 84 overall, with an 85 offense and 83 defense, and reports $97.8 million in cap room. The money suggests flexibility. The roster count says decisions are due.

Sauce Gardner's quad strain and Kapena Gushiken's ACL sprain are both listed as one-game injuries, with expected returns in Preseason Week 2. Neither is on injured reserve. These are not reasons to panic; they are reminders that depth charts are living documents, not posters.

One should resist the temptation to call the Bison finished before they have played a game under their new name.

A grade is not a guarantee

My A− is a judgment about the quality and ambition of the work completed, not a prediction that every transaction will look brilliant by December. A 10–11-win projection reflects the ceiling I see and the risks I cannot ignore.

If the Bison struggle, it will not automatically mean the vision was foolish. If they win, it will not erase the price paid in Indianapolis or the draft assets sent elsewhere. Serious analysis must hold more than one thought at a time.

Oklahoma City has a compelling football team and a complicated history. The first season will tell us whether it also has a contender.

He isn't waiting for the organization to tell him it's ready. He's trying to make it ready.

Mercer's verdict: A−. Projection: 10–11 wins. This roster can contend. The burden now is proving that the offseason's promise survives the season.
